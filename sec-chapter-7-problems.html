    Example 10. Geometric Series and Divergence Test.

    For each series given below, either find the exact value (sum) of the series or explain in a sentence or two why the series diverges. When giving the exact value of a series, you do not need to simplify.
    🔗
    1. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^2+20n}{10n^2-5}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle 3+\frac{3}{\pi}+\frac{3}{\pi^2}+\frac{3}{\pi^3}+\frac{3}{\pi^4}+\ldots\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}+\frac{1}{\pi}(2)+\frac{1}{\pi}(2)^2+\frac{1}{\pi}(2)^3+\frac{1}{\pi}(2)^4+\ldots\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. The general term is \(a_n=\frac{n^2+20n}{10n^2-5}\text{.}\) Here, we have
      \begin{align*} \lim_{n\to\infty} a_n \amp = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{n^2+20n}{10n^2-5} \cdot \frac{\frac{1}{n^2}}{\frac{1}{n^2}}\\ \amp = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1+\frac{20}{n}}{10-\frac{5}{n^2}}\\ \amp = \frac{1+0}{10-0}\\ \amp = \frac{1}{10} \neq 0 \end{align*}
      Since \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n\neq0\text{,}\) by the Divergence Test (\(n\)th Term Test), the series diverges.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. This is a geometric series, \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty3\left(\frac{1}{\pi}\right)^n\text{,}\) with \(a=3\) and \(r=\frac{1}{\pi}\text{.}\) Since the common ratio \(r\) satisfies \(|r|=\frac{1}{\pi}<1\text{,}\) this series is a convergent geometric series with sum
      \begin{equation*} \sum_{n=0}^\infty 3 \left(\frac{1}{\pi}\right)^n = \frac{a}{1-r} = \frac{3}{1-\frac{1}{\pi}} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. This is a geometric series. Note that each thing being added is \(\frac{1}{\pi}\) multiplied by \(2\) to some power. This gives us a clue that here, \(a=\frac{1}{\pi}\text{,}\) and the common ratio is \(r=2\text{.}\) Since \(|r|=2>1\text{,}\) this means that our geometric series diverges.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 11. Integral Test.

    Consider the following series:
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \; \frac{1}{n^3} \end{equation*}
    First, show that the Integral Test is applicable to this series by verifying that all requirements in the statement of the Integral Test are satisfied. Then justify whether the series converges or diverges according to the Integral Test (which you may do even if you are unable to verify the requirements of the Integral Test). You must use the Integral Test to receive credit. Show all work and summarize your final answer in a complete sentence to earn full credit.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(f(x)=\frac{1}{x^3}\) which is continuous and positive (since \(x\ge1\)). Moreover, \(f(x)=\frac{1}{x^3}\) is decreasing because the denominator, \(x^3\text{,}\) is increasing for \(x\ge1\text{.}\) (Alternatively, observe that \(f'(x)=-3x^{-4}=-3\cdot\frac{1}{x^4}\) is negative for \(x\ge1\text{.}\)) Therefore, all requirements of the Integral Test are satisfied.
    🔗
    Applying the Integral Test, consider \(\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\frac{1}{x^3}\,dx\text{,}\) which we know converges by the \(p\)-Test for improper integrals with \(p=3\text{.}\) Specifically,
    \begin{align*} \int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x^3} \, dx \amp = \lim_{b \to \infty} \int_1^b \frac{1}{x^3} \, dx\\ \amp = \lim_{b \to \infty} \left( -\frac{1}{2x^2} \right) \Big|_1^b\\ \amp = \lim_{b \to \infty} \left( -\frac{1}{2b^2} + \frac{1}{2(1)^2} \right)\\ \amp = 0 + \frac{1}{2} < \infty \end{align*}
    Therefore, by the integral test, we conclude that \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^3}\) converges.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 12. Series Convergence Tests.

    Two series are given below. For each series, do both of the following to receive full credit:
    • Determine whether the series converges or diverges by applying one of our convergence tests. You must clearly show that all requirements in the statement of the convergence test are satisfied.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Fully summarize your conclusion in a sentence or two, clearly indicating which convergence test you used and the result. Then explain why you chose to use said test based on the series.
      🔗
      🔗
    Note: If you are comparing to another series using the DCT or LCT, you may argue whether that comparison series converges or diverges using a \(p\)-Series Test.
    🔗
    1. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{n^4}{n^6+n^3+1}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{2^{2n+1}}{n}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. For large \(n\text{,}\) the series behaves like \(\frac{n^4}{n^6}=\frac{1}{n^2}\text{.}\) We will apply the LCT. First, set \(a_n=\frac{n^4}{n^6+n^3+1}\text{,}\) \(b_n = \frac{1}{n^2}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      Both \(a_n>0\) and \(b_n>0\) for all \(n\ge1\) - both are fractions with always positive numerator and denominator - so LCT applies.
      🔗
      We now check the limit:
      \begin{align*} \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{a_n}{b_n} \amp = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{\frac{n^4}{n^6+n^3+1}}{\frac{1}{n^2}}\\ \amp = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{n^4}{n^6+n^3+1} \cdot \frac{n^2}{1}\\ \amp = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{n^6}{n^6+n^3+1} \cdot \frac{\frac{1}{n^6}}{\frac{1}{n^6}}\\ \amp = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{n^3}+\frac{1}{n^6}}\\ \amp = 1\text{.} \end{align*}
      Since \(\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{b_n}=1\) is a positive number, LCT says that \(\sum a_n\) and \(\sum b_n\) converge/diverge together.
      🔗
      \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^2}\) converges by the \(p\)-Series Test (with \(p=2>1\)), so \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^4}{n^6+n^3+1}\) also converges by LCT.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Here we will apply the Ratio Test:
      \begin{align*} L = \lim_{n\to\infty} \left| \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} \right| \amp = \lim_{n\to\infty} \left| \frac{2^{2(n+1)+1}}{n+1} \cdot \frac{n}{2^{2n+1}} \right|\\ \amp = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{2^{2n+3}}{2^{2n+1}} \cdot \frac{n}{n+1}\\ \amp = \lim_{n\to\infty} 2^2 \cdot \frac{n}{n+1}\\ \amp = 4 \cdot \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{n}{n+1} \cdot \frac{\frac{1}{n}}{\frac{1}{n}}\\ \amp = 4 \cdot \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{n}}\\ \amp = 4 \cdot \frac{1}{1+0} = 4 \end{align*}
      Using the Ratio Test, since \(L>1\text{,}\) the series diverges.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 13. Alternating Series.

    Determine whether the following series is absolutely convergent, conditionally convergent, or divergent. You must show all work to receive credit.
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \; \frac{(-1)^n}{6n} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, we check for absolute convergence:
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left| \frac{(-1)^n}{6n} \right| = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{6n} = \frac{1}{6} \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n} \end{equation*}
    \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\) diverges by \(p\)-Series Test (with \(p=1\text{,}\) so \(p\leq1\)), so the series is not absolutely convergent.
    🔗
    Next, we check for conditional convergence. We will apply the Alternating Series Test. Check requirements (using \(a_n=\frac{1}{6n}\)):
    • Is the sequence of terms decreasing? Use \(f(x)=\frac{1}{6x}\text{,}\) then \(f'(x)=-\frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{1}{x^2}\text{,}\) which is negative for all \(x\geq1\text{,}\) so \(f(x)\) is decreasing.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Is the limit of the terms equal to zero?
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{6n} = \frac{1}{6} \cdot \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n} = \frac{1}{6} \cdot 0 = 0 \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    The AST applies and therefore \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{6n}\) converges. The series converges conditionally since the sequence \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{6n}\) was divergent.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 14. Power Series.

    1. Determine the radius of convergence of the following power series:
      \begin{equation*} \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(n+1)\cdot(x-10)^n}{5^n} \end{equation*}
      You must show all work to receive full credit and be sure to clearly indicate your final answer. Note: You do not need to determine the interval of convergence, only the radius.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Consider an arbitrary power series and a fact about the power series:
      \begin{equation*} \sum_{k=0}^\infty c_k (x-10)^k \end{equation*}
      🔗
      Fact: The power series converges at \(x=2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      In a sentence or two, explain why the following Claim must be true, must be false, or why there is not enough information to determine whether it is true or false:
      🔗
      Claim: The power series diverges at \(x=18\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. We will apply the Ratio Test:
      \begin{align*} L \amp = \lim_{n\to\infty} \left| \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} \right|\\ \amp = \lim_{n\to\infty} \left| \frac{((n+1)+1) \cdot (x-10)^{n+1}}{5^{n+1}} \cdot \frac{5^n}{(n+1)(x-10)^n} \right|\\ \amp = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{5} \cdot |x-10| \cdot \frac{n+2}{n+1}\\ \amp = \frac{1}{5} |x-10| \cdot \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{n+2}{n+1} \cdot \frac{\frac{1}{n}}{\frac{1}{n}}\\ \amp = \frac{1}{5} |x-10| \cdot \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1+\frac{2}{n}}{1+\frac{1}{n}}\\ \amp = \frac{1}{5} |x-10| \cdot \frac{1+0}{1+0} = \frac{1}{5} |x-10| \end{align*}
      The endpoints of the interval of convergence are when \(L=1\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{5} |x-10| = 1 \qquad \implies \qquad |x-10| = 5 \end{equation*}
      This is solved when \(x=15\) (when \(x-10=5\)) or when \(x=5\) (when \(x-10=-5\)).
      🔗
      The interval of convergence includes \(5<x<15\text{;}\) the endpoints \(x=5\) and \(x=15\) may or may not be included. But the radius of convergence is half the length of the interval, which is \(\frac{10}{2}=5\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The Radius of Convergence (RoC) is at least \(8\text{.}\) The RoC could equal \(8\text{,}\) then the right endpoint of the Interval of Convergence (IoC) would be \(10+8=18\text{.}\) However, the series may or may not converge at each endpoint, so there is not enough information.
      🔗
      🔗

    Example 15. Modifying Taylor Series.

    Recall that \(\cos(x)\) has the following Taylor series (centered at \(x=0\)), valid for all \(-\infty\le x\le\infty\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \cos(x) = 1 - \frac{x^2}{2!} + \frac{x^4}{4!} - \frac{x^6}{6!} + \ldots = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^k x^{2k}}{(2k)!} \end{equation*}
    Using the Taylor series for \(\cos(x)\text{,}\) give the Taylor series (centered at \(x=0\)) for
    \begin{equation*} f(x) = x^3 \; \cos(x^2) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{align*} \cos(x) \amp = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^kx^{2k}}{(2k)!}\\ \amp \Downarrow\\ \cos(x^2) \amp = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^k(x^2)^{2k}}{(2k)!} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^kx^{4k}}{(2k)!}\\ \amp \Downarrow\\ x^3\cdot\cos(x^2) \amp = x^3 \cdot \left( \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^kx^{4k}}{(2k)!} \right) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^kx^{4k+3}}{(2k)!} \end{align*}
    🔗

    Example 16. Taylor Series and Derivatives.

    A Taylor series (centered at \(x=0\)) for an unspecified function \(g(x)\) is given as follows:
    \begin{equation*} g(x) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{2}{(k+2)!} x^k \end{equation*}
    Determine the exact value of \(g^{(11)}(0)\text{.}\) You do not need to simplify your answer.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The general formula for a Taylor series is given by
    \begin{equation*} g(x) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{g^{(k)}(0)}{k!} x^k \end{equation*}
    Which term includes something like \(g^{(11)}(0)\text{?}\) Use \(k=11\) in the general formula:
    \begin{equation*} g(x) = \ldots + \frac{g^{(11)}(0)}{11!} x^{11} + \ldots \end{equation*}
    Now compare this with the specific coefficient on the \(x^{11}\) term of \(g(x)\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} g(x) \amp = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{2}{(k+2)!} x^k\\ \amp = \ldots + \frac{2}{13!} x^{11} + \ldots \end{align*}
    Pulling things together, we can solve:
    \begin{align*} \frac{g^{(11)}(0)}{11!} \amp = \frac{2}{13!}\\ g^{(11)}(0) \amp = \frac{2\cdot11!}{13!} = \frac{2}{13\cdot12} \end{align*}
    🔗
